What to fix first

  1. Cache what a turn asks the rules for. The AI's own code is a sliver of the time; its turns are long because the same expected-damage and threat estimates are recomputed for every square and target, and each one rebuilds the weapon's profile and re-collects its combat effects and auras. Within one decision nothing those depend on changes. Caching a unit's resolved weapon profiles and threat values for the length of a decision should cut most of the —% that sits under the weapon profile.
  2. Don't redo the turn set-up when nothing changed. Posture, the threat matrix and the missives run at the start of every turn, —% of turn time, even for a pass. They depend on the board, so they can be kept until a unit moves, attacks or dies. The slowest turns of all are Movement Phase turns that end in a pass, up to — ms: the AI works out a destination for every unit it could move, one after another, and then moves none. A unit that found nothing worth doing could be skipped until the board changes.
  3. Keep the page answering during an AI turn. A turn runs as one task on the main thread, up to — ms. Splitting the search into steps that yield between units, or running the AI in a Web Worker, would keep the board and buttons responsive while it thinks.
  4. Update the unit list instead of rebuilding it. It is cleared and rebuilt from HTML about — times a battle, the single largest function of its own in the profile. Redrawing only the rows that changed, or once per animation frame, removes most of it and most of the DOM churn.
  5. Spend fewer turns idle. —% of unit-rounds had nothing legal in reach. Part of that is the game (a walk costs no action point), but it is also melee units stopping short and guns ending moves out of sight; the Movement Phase could value ending where the unit can act. And —% of charges met an empty square because the target had not yet moved and Fell Back: a charge on a unit that has already moved cannot be escaped.
  6. Price what the commands buy before buying them. —% of CP went on uses that changed nothing the record can see. Double Time when the square was already in reach and Secure Objective that flipped no objective are the AI's valuation; Miracles and protective spells are partly the record's blind spot.

How this was measured

Each battle was played in the built game page by the game's own AI on both seats, with the battle-report tool (scripts/battle_report/), its new --lean --profile mode: the AI's decisions timed in the page around the engine's own functions, memory and the page's metrics read over the browser's DevTools protocol at each Battle Round's start, and a CPU profile sampled every millisecond. The timing changes no rule: a battle replayed with it on ends exactly as it did without. Lean mode leaves out the report's counterfactual records, which run inside the AI's calls and would inflate its times.

Machine: —, — cores, — GB, — headless. Two battles ran at once, each in its own browser, so a player's faster machine would see shorter turns; the shares and the memory figures carry over. The AI's own pauses (its "thinking" and action delays) were set to zero, so the times are pure decision time.

Decision quality uses the timed battles plus two 12-battle batches with full records, all on the same engine. "Bought nothing" means the record found no attack it improved, no target it made shootable, no move only it put in reach, no objective it decided and no damage it caused; a Miracle's attack option and a shield nobody attacked through cannot be priced, so their rows overstate the waste.
